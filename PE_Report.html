Evaluating PATH (Physical Activity to Thrive & Heal) Training in Schools: Preliminary Findings

Author

Paul Yuergens

Program Context and Objectives

In the wake of COVID-related school closures, children experienced reduced physical activity and increased rates of obesity, highlighting the need for effective physical education professional development (Lange et al. 2021). To address this, the State of Greatlakesia launched PATH (Physical Activity to Thrive & Heal), a Physical Education Professional Development (PEPD) program designed to improve physical activity and well-being among students. PATH offers training for a variety of school professionals in both synchronous and asynchronous formats, allowing flexibility for participants.

Although a randomized control trial is planned to evaluate student outcomes, preliminary feedback is being collected from teachers before and after completing the training. These early data allow the program to assess differences between synchronous and asynchronous modalities and provide a quasi-experimental perspective on training effectiveness.

Data Collection and Analysis Approach

Surveys were administered to school staff before and after the PATH training, and participants were categorized into four roles:

  • Classroom Educators (CE)
  • Physical Education Teachers (PET)
  • Administrators (ADMIN)
  • Other Student Support Roles (OSS)

The PATH training is informed by the STEP (Standards for Training, Exercise, & Physical Education) Institute’s five categories of physical fitness:

  • Strength
  • Cardio
  • Flexibility & Mobility
  • Diet & Nutrition
  • Adapted Physical Activity & Technology (APAT)

Although APAT is included in the STEP framework, research suggests that traditional physical education preparation inadequately addresses adapted physical education content (Laughlin 2024). In addition, Teamwork & Cooperation was included to complement the physical domains. While physical education settings naturally involve group activities, deliberately facilitating collaboration and communication requires student-centered pedagogical approaches that pose challenges for pre-service and in-service teachers not typically trained in these methods (Silva, Farias, and Mesquita 2021).

Survey items used a 4-point Likert scale and were aggregated into six indices corresponding to these categories.

Because random assignment was not possible, pre- and post-surveys were paired using Levenshtein distance to control for individual differences; matches were verified manually (Levenshtein 1966). The Qualtrics survey data were prepared for analysis with a data dictionary incorporated into the cleaning process (Lewis 2022).

Assumptions and Reliability

Normality checks on the difference scores (post − pre) showed nearly symmetric distributions (skewness = -0.04 to 0.07) and near-normal kurtosis (2.77-3.19), with Shapiro-Wilk tests indicating significant departures from normality (all p < .001). However, given the large sample sizes (n = 384 asynchronous, n = 2,016 synchronous), Welch’s two-sample t-test was appropriate for comparing the two groups.

All indices demonstrated good to excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.81–0.91), with the Teamwork & Cooperation scale showing particularly strong reliability (α = 0.91). Average inter-item correlations ranged from 0.59 to 0.62, indicating appropriate item homogeneity without excessive redundancy. Within each index, item–total correlations were consistently strong (all r ≥ 0.66), and no items showed evidence warranting removal. Reliability was evaluated separately for each domain given the multidimensional structure of the instrument. These results indicate that the composite scores for Strength, Cardio, Flexibility & Mobility, Diet & Nutrition, APAT, and Teamwork & Cooperation reliably capture their intended constructs, providing a stable basis for examining pre–post changes and differences across delivery modalities or professional roles.

Analysis

Comparing Outcomes Across Training Delivery Modalities

Welch two-sample t-tests were used to compare asynchronous (n = 384) and synchronous (n = 2,016) training groups across all five STEP categories and Teamwork & Cooperation. No statistically significant differences were observed (p values ranged from 0.080 to 0.826), indicating that outcomes did not vary meaningfully by training delivery format. Accordingly, the two groups were combined for subsequent analyses to increase statistical power and simplify interpretation.

Differences in Training Gains Across Professional Roles

Linear regression models were estimated with pre–post change scores as the outcome and role indicators as predictors, using Physical Education Teachers as the reference group. Robust (HC1) standard errors were applied to account for potential heteroskedasticity, following applied econometric guidance on nonstandard standard error issues (Angrist and Pischke 2009).

All professional roles demonstrated significant pre-post improvements across outcome domains. Physical Education Teachers (reference group) showed significant gains ranging from 0.077 (Cardio) to 0.23 (Teamwork & Cooperation), with the exception of Flexibility & Mobility, which did not reach statistical significance. Compared to PE Teachers, Classroom Educators demonstrated additional improvements across all domains, with differences ranging from 0.102 (Teamwork & Cooperation, p < .001) to 0.343 (Flexibility & Mobility, p < .001). Administrators and Other Student Support staff exhibited similar patterns, showing significantly greater gains than PE Teachers across all domains. These findings reflect variation in baseline exposure to PE-related practices, with non-PE professionals showing larger improvements consistent with addressing knowledge gaps not typically covered in their prior training or professional experience.

Among participants without prior PEPD, the pattern of differential gains became more pronounced. Physical Education Teachers in this subsample showed gains ranging from 0.022 to 0.219 across domains, though gains in Flexibility & Mobility and Diet & Nutrition did not reach conventional significance levels. Relative to these Physical Education Teachers, Classroom Educators showed additional gains of 0.149–0.444 (p < .001 across all domains), Administrators showed additional gains of 0.166–0.442 (p < .001 across all domains), and Other Student Support staff showed additional gains of 0.116–0.440 (p < .01 to p < .001). The largest relative gains appeared in Flexibility & Mobility across all non-PE roles (0.440–0.444, p < .001). These results highlight that participants lacking prior PEPD exposure showed the greatest responsiveness to training, with the program effectively addressing content gaps not typically covered in traditional Physical Education instruction.

To aid interpretation of the regression results, Table 4 presents descriptive pre- and post-training mean scores by professional role and outcome domain. Visual inspection of these changes reveals an important pattern: while all professional roles showed improvements, the magnitude of gains was modest across all groups. Physical Education Teachers, who entered with higher baseline scores (M = 2.63–3.38), demonstrated small improvements (0.02–0.22 points, representing approximately 1–6% of the scale range). Classroom Educators, Administrators, and Other Student Support staff—who started at similar baseline levels (M = 2.42–2.62)—showed somewhat larger gains (0.34–0.46 points, approximately 9–12% of scale range), though these too represent modest practical effects. This pattern underscores that with large samples (n ≈ 1,444), statistical significance does not necessarily indicate large practical effects. However, non-PE professionals consistently gained more than PE Teachers, suggesting the training addressed specific knowledge gaps for those with limited prior PE exposure, even if the magnitude of improvement was moderate.

Discussion

This study examined the effectiveness of the PATH training program across 2,400 school personnel using a pre-post design. Three key findings emerged.

First, training delivery modality (asynchronous vs. synchronous) did not significantly affect outcomes (all p > .05), suggesting flexible implementation without compromising effectiveness.

Second, while most professional roles demonstrated statistically significant improvements across competency domains, practical significance was modest. Physical Education Teachers did not show significant gains in Flexibility & Mobility, and several gains reached only p < .05. Gains averaged 0.33–0.39 points on the 4-point scale (approximately 8–10% of scale range). With large samples, even small effects achieve statistical significance, highlighting the importance of interpreting results beyond p-values alone.

Third, non-PE professionals (Classroom Educators, Administrators, Other Student Support) showed significantly greater gains than Physical Education Teachers (additional 0.09–0.36 points, p < .01 to p < .001). While statistically significant, this difference represents approximately 2–9% of the scale range—a modest practical difference. This pattern suggests the training addressed knowledge gaps for those with limited PE background, though the magnitude of benefit was small. Whether such gains translate to meaningful changes in practice or student outcomes remains an open question.

These findings illustrate the tension between statistical and practical significance in large-scale professional development research. Future work should examine whether training effects persist over time and whether they lead to observable changes in professional practice.
